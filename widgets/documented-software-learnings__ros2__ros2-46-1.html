Chapter 46: robot position and the TF2 library

Where Is the Robot? Frames and the TF2 Library

The robot can now be moved freely with a joystick, and that raises a new question: where is the robot in its environment? This page follows the answer in two parts: what it means to know the position of a robot, with the three frames R, O and M, and the ROS library TF2 that works with frames, with the robot model as a tree of frames that robot_state_publisher publishes on /tf. Every lesson has something to move and watch. All numbers on the page are teaching values.

Words used on this page (open this if a word is new)
velocity commandA wanted linear speed V and a wanted angular speed ω, sent to the robot for example by a joystick.
poseWhere the robot is and which way it faces on the plane: x, y and θ.
reference frameA set of axes attached to something, used to give positions as numbers.
frame RThe frame attached to the robot. It moves along with the robot.
frame OThe frame placed at the starting position of the robot, where the motion started.
frame MA global frame that is fixed with the map. It never moves.
transformationHow far one frame is moved (x and y) and how much it is turned (θ) compared with another frame.
transformation matrixA table of numbers that holds a transformation. The first two columns hold the turn and the last column holds the position.
R seen from OThe transformation that connects the robot frame R to the initial frame O. It is the position of the robot relative to its start.
R seen from MThe transformation that connects R to the map frame M. It is the position of the robot relative to the map.
conventionAn agreement that everybody follows. In ROS the position of the robot is given with three frames: R, O and M.
TF and TF2The ROS library that keeps and uses the frames of a robot, calculates transformations between them and tracks them over time. TF2 is its latest version and is used in this course.
treeFrames connected from a parent to its children, with no loops. The model of a robot is a tree of frames.
linkA part of the robot in the URDF file. Every link has its own frame.
jointThe connection between a parent link and a child link. It says where the child frame is placed compared with the parent.
fixed jointA joint that never moves. The frame of the child stays at the same place compared with the parent.
URDFThe file that describes the model of the robot: its links and the joints between them.
nodeA small program that runs inside ROS.
topicA named channel on which nodes publish messages for other nodes to read.
robot_state_publisherThe node that reads the URDF, calculates the transformations between all the frames and publishes them on /tf.
/tfThe topic that carries the transformations between frames.
RVizThe program that shows the robot and its frames on the screen.

PART 1

Where is the robot?

Once a robot can be moved with velocity commands, knowing its position in the environment becomes necessary. But the sentence “the position of the robot” can be understood in more than one way.

01

The joystick moves the robot, but where is it?

The joystick sends velocity commands: a linear speed and an angular speed. The robot moves, but the commands do not say where it is. Press Next to go step by step, and Back to return. Press Play and use the sliders.

Key idea. Velocity commands move the robot, but they do not tell where it is. Knowing the position in the environment is a new need.
02

What does the position of the robot mean? Frames R, O and M

The position can mean where the robot is relative to its starting position, or where it is relative to a fixed point of the map. To remove the ambiguity, ROS uses a convention with three frames. Press Next to go step by step, and Back to return. Move the sliders to change the robot or the start, and touch a coloured name to light it up in the picture.

Key idea. Three frames: R moves with the robot, O is the starting position and M is fixed with the map. Knowing the position means knowing R seen from O and O seen from M.
PART 2

The TF2 library

Again frames are used and transformation matrices are calculated. ROS has a library for exactly this job. It was already used in this course, when the model of the robot was created and visualised.

03

TF2 and its three jobs

TF2 keeps and uses the reference frames of a robot, calculates the transformation between different frames, and tracks the frames over time. Press Next to go step by step, and Back to return. Press Play to let the robot move, or move the time slider.

Key idea. TF2 works with frames: it keeps and uses them, calculates the transformation between any two of them and tracks them over time.
04

The robot model is a tree of frames

The URDF model of the robot can be seen as a tree made of several interconnected reference frames. Press Next to go step by step, and Back to return. Touch a name to find its frame on the robot, and slide z to move base_link.

Key idea. The robot model is a tree of frames. Each joint connects a parent frame to a child frame.
05

robot_state_publisher and the topic /tf

The node robot_state_publisher reads and parses the URDF, extracts all the links and so all the frames, calculates the transformation matrices among all of them and publishes them on the topic /tf. The robot can then be visualised. Press Next to go step by step, and Back to return.

Key idea. robot_state_publisher turns the URDF into transformations and publishes them on /tf. Tools such as RViz read /tf to show the robot.
06

Summary and quiz

Key takeaways

  1. A joystick sends velocity commands. They do not say where the robot is, so knowing the position in the environment is a new need.
  2. Knowing the position can mean the position relative to the starting position, or relative to a fixed point of the map. The word alone is ambiguous.
  3. The ROS convention uses three frames: R moves with the robot, O is the starting position and M is a global frame fixed with the map.
  4. Knowing the position means knowing the transformation between R and O, and the transformation between O and M.
  5. All of this is again a matter of reference frames and transformation matrices. The ROS library for them is TF, and its latest version, TF2, is used in this course.
  6. TF2 keeps and uses the frames, calculates transformations between different frames and tracks the frames over time.
  7. The model of the robot (URDF) is a tree of interconnected frames. A joint connects a parent frame to a child frame.
  8. robot_state_publisher reads the URDF, extracts the links and so the frames, calculates the transformations among them and publishes them on the topic /tf. That is how the robot is visualised.

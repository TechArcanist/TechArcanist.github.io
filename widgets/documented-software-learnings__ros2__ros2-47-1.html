Chapter 47: chains of frames and the order of multiplication

Where Is the Person? Chaining Frames and Multiplying in Order

This page answers one question: where is a person that a robot sees, on the map? The robot has a camera, the camera sees the person, and the robot knows where it is on the map. Joining these pieces one after another gives the answer. The pieces must be joined in the right order, and a turning box shows why. Every lesson has something to move and watch. The numbers are only examples, and the pictures are drawn from above to keep them easy to read.

Words used on this page (open this if a word is new)
reference frameA set of axes attached to something, used to give positions as numbers.
transformationHow far one frame is moved (x and y) and how much it is turned (θ) compared with another frame.
transformation matrixA table of numbers that holds a transformation. The first two columns hold the turn and the last column holds the position.
base_footprint (frame B)The first frame of the robot in its model. On this page it is called B.
camera_link (frame C)The frame of the camera in the model of the robot. On this page it is called C.
person_link (frame P)The frame that the person recognition application attaches to a detected person. On this page it is called P.
map_link (frame M)A global frame attached to the map of the world. On this page it is called M.
T with two namesThe transformation that connects the bottom frame to the top frame. The top name is the frame the numbers are measured from, and the bottom name is the frame that is described.
URDFThe file that describes the model of the robot: its links and how they are connected.
chain of framesFrames connected one after the other, so that the path from the first frame to the last one goes through all of them.
product of matricesCombining two transformations into one overall transformation. It gives the pose reached by doing the first transformation and then the second.
order of the productThe sequence in which the matrices are multiplied. It must follow the chain from the first frame to the last, in the order the frames appear.
localization applicationA ROS 2 application that provides the position of the robot on the map.
parallelepipedA box shape, like a brick.
x, y and z axesThree straight lines at right angles to each other, used to give positions in 3D. A turn is described by the axis it goes around and by the angle.

01

Frames turn many problems into one question

A frame is a pair of arrows (x and y) stuck on something, so that we can say where things are. A robot has many frames: one on its body, one on each wheel, one on the camera. Many robot problems become one question: where is one frame compared with another, and how much is it turned? Press Next for the next step and Back to go back. Press Play to see it move.

Key idea. Many robotics problems are the same question: how does the transformation between two reference frames change over time? That covers the rotation of a wheel and the position of the robot on the map.
02

A chain of frames: person, camera, robot and map

A robot rarely knows everything. Here, the robot model knows where the camera is on the robot. A person-finding program knows where the person is compared with the camera. A localization program knows where the robot is on the map. Nobody tells where the person is on the map, but it can be worked out by joining these pieces one after another (this joining is called multiplying). Press Next and Back to move between steps, and use the sliders.

Key idea. The transformation between two frames that nobody provides is found by multiplying the known ones along the chain: B to P is B to C times C to P, and M to P is M to B times B to C times C to P.
03

Walking the chain: one move after another

Each piece of the join is one small move that starts where the move before it ended. Doing the moves one after another ends at the person. Press Next and Back to move between steps, and use the sliders.

Key idea. Each piece is measured from the frame before it. Doing the pieces one after another from the first frame ends at the person, seen from the map.
04

The order of the product matters

The pieces must be joined in the right order. The same moves in a different order end in a different place. A box turned in 3D shows this. Press Next and Back to move between steps, and use the sliders and Play.

Key idea. The same two moves or turns in a different order end in a different place. So transformation matrices must always be multiplied in the order of the chain.
05

Summary and quiz

Key takeaways

  1. A robot can be represented as reference frames linked together. Many problems then become the calculation of the transformation between two or more frames.
  2. Knowing the rotation of a wheel means knowing how the transformation from the robot frame to the wheel frame changes over time. Knowing the position of the robot on the map means the same for the robot frame and the map frame.
  3. Often only partial information is available. In the example, the URDF gives the camera frame with respect to the robot, an application gives the person with respect to the camera, and a localization application gives the robot with respect to the map.
  4. The position of the person with respect to the robot is not given by anyone, but it is the product of the camera with respect to the robot and the person with respect to the camera.
  5. In the same way, the position of the person on the map is the product of three matrices: map to robot, robot to camera, camera to person.
  6. The order of the product is very important. The matrices must be multiplied by following the chain from the initial frame to the final frame, in the order the frames appear.
  7. A box turned 90° about Z and then 90° about Y ends in a completely different position than when turned about Y first and then about Z.

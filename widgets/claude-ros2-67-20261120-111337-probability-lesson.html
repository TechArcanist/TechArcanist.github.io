Chapter 67: probability for robots

Probability Introduction Why a robot is never completely sure, and how numbers from 0 to 1 help

Chapters 58 to 66 worked out where the robot is by adding up wheel readings, as if every reading were perfect. This page shows why that can never be fully true, and starts a new way of thinking: instead of one answer, give every possible place a share between 0 and 1. The maths needed is only counting, dividing and adding. Every number is built step by step from a coin, a die and a rock on a path.

Words used on this page (open this if a word is new)
uncertaintyNot knowing something exactly. A robot always has some.
environmentEverything around the robot: floor, weather, rocks, puddles.
actuatorA part that moves the robot, such as a motor and its wheel.
sensorA part that measures something, such as an encoder or a rock detector.
noiseA small random error added to a reading.
odometry modelThe formulas and sizes (wheel radius, wheel separation) used to work out the pose.
slipA wheel that turns without moving the robot the same distance.
deterministicGiving one single answer, as if it were certain.
probabilisticKeeping every possible answer, each with a share between 0 and 1.
probabilityA number from 0 to 1 that says how likely something is.
shareThe probability of one possible place. All the shares add up to 1.
points (weight)A rough score before dividing by the total.
trustHow often the detector is right, for example 0.8 means 4 times out of 5. Assumed in this page.
normaliseDivide every score by the total so that everything adds up to 1.
cellOne short piece of the path, numbered 1 to 12 here.

Why a robot is never completely sure

A robot told to stop at exactly 2 m never lands on exactly the same spot twice. Four different things push it off, and each one is small. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Doubt comes from four places: the environment, the actuators (motors and wheels), the sensors and the software model such as odometry. Each adds a small random push, so the same command gives a spread of results, not one result.

02

A small wrong size becomes a wrong position

Odometry trusts the sizes in its formulas: the wheel radius and the distance between the wheels. If one of them is a little wrong, or a wheel slips, the position is wrong, and the error grows. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. A wheel radius that is 1 mm too small or too large gives about 3% error in every distance, so 6 cm over 2 m and 30 cm over 10 m. A wrong wheel separation turns the heading wrong. Slip makes the robot believe it drives straight while it curves.

One answer or a list of answers

A robot moves along a path with three rocks and has a detector that says rock when it is next to one. Where is it? One way gives a single answer. The other keeps a list of answers. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. A deterministic answer pins the robot to one place and ignores that the detector can fail, is not exact, and that other rocks exist. A probabilistic answer keeps every possible place, a higher share for the likely ones and a small but not zero share for the rest.

04

Probability: a number from 0 to 1

Probability is a number that says how likely something is. It is built from counting, so a coin and a die are enough to see all the rules. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Probability = (ways it can happen) ÷ (all ways). 0 means it cannot happen, 1 means it surely happens, and the probabilities of all the possible outcomes add up to 1.

05

A detector reading changes the shares

Start with equal shares for every cell of the path. Then the rock detector says rock. Cells next to a rock become more likely, the others less likely, and none becomes impossible. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. New share = (old share × how well the cell fits the reading) ÷ the total of all of these. Dividing by the total makes the shares add up to 1 again. A detector that can fail leaves every cell a small share above 0.

06

Move and read again

The robot drives 3 cells forward and the detector says no rock. The shares shift forward with the robot, the movement itself can be uncertain, and the new reading makes the fitting cells more likely. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Each new reading sharpens the picture: move the shares with the robot, multiply by how well each cell fits the new reading, divide by the total. After a few readings one cell usually stands far above the rest, and the robot makes better assumptions about where it is.

Summary and quiz

Key takeaways

  1. A robot is never completely sure. The environment, the actuators, the sensors and the software model (such as odometry) each add a small error.
  2. Small errors grow: a wheel radius wrong by 1 mm gives about 3% error in every distance, and wheel slip bends a straight drive into a curve.
  3. A deterministic answer pins the robot to one place. It ignores that the detector can fail, is not exact, and that there are other rocks.
  4. A probabilistic answer gives every possible place a share between 0 and 1, higher for the likely places and small but not zero for the others.
  5. Probability = ways it can happen ÷ all ways. 0 means impossible, 1 means certain, and all shares add up to 1.
  6. A reading updates the shares: new share = old share × how well the cell fits the reading, divided by the total of all of these.
  7. With a detector that is right 4 times out of 5 (assumed), 3 rocks on a 12 cell path and a reading of rock, each rock cell gets 0.19 and every other cell 0.05.
  8. After moving 3 cells and reading no rock, the shares shift forward and the cell 12 stands at 0.33, far above the rest. More readings sharpen the picture.

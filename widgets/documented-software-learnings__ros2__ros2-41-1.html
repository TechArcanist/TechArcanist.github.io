Differential kinematics

Wheels to World: The Velocity of a Mobile Robot

A mobile robot with two wheels moves because its wheels turn. This page follows the whole path in six parts: how the velocity of a robot is described, the linear velocity from the wheels, the angular velocity from the wheels, the velocity in the world frame, the forward differential kinematics that puts everything together, and a simple speed controller that drives the robot with a joystick. Every step has a simulator to play with. All numbers on the page are teaching values.

PART 1

Velocity of a robot

Before the differential kinematics equation, the velocity of a robot in a two dimensional reference frame has to be described mathematically. It starts from the pose and from a new variable: the time.

01

The pose of the robot

To describe the velocity of a robot, the start is its pose. The pose has three components. The first two, x and y, indicate the position of the robot in the two dimensional space. The third one, θ, indicates its orientation on the plane.

Key idea. The pose P is made of three numbers: x and y for the position, θ for the orientation.
02

The pose at two moments in time

To switch from the position to the velocity, a new variable is needed: the time. The velocity can be considered as a sequence of positions of the robot over time. A snapshot is taken at time t0, giving the pose P0. At a later time t1 a new snapshot is taken: the robot has moved, rotated and translated, and the new pose is P1.

Key idea. Snapshots at two times give two poses, P0 at t0 and P1 at t1.
03

Why does subtracting give a velocity?

The velocity comes from comparing the two poses: new minus old, divided by the time. This lesson shows why, one small step at a time, with the numbers of the x position, the y position and the angle. Press Next to move on and Back to return. Touch or hover a coloured number to see where it is in the picture.

Key idea. A velocity is a change per second. Subtract the old value from the new one to get the change, then divide by the time taken.
04

The velocity: space divided by time

By definition, the velocity is the ratio between space and time. So for each component of the pose, the derivative with respect to time can be calculated. This gives the velocity of the robot, which is also made of three variables: ẋ and ẏ are the linear velocity of the robot in the plane, and θ̇ is its angular velocity.

Key idea. Ṗ has three parts: ẋ and ẏ are the linear velocity, θ̇ is the angular velocity. Each one is a change in the pose divided by the time.
05

First the units: what do rad/s and m/s mean?

The wheels are described in rad/s and the robot in m/s. Before using them in equations, this lesson shows in 3D what these units mean: what a radian is, what turning at so many radians per second looks like, how that becomes metres per second on the floor, and how the turning of the wheels differs from the turning of the whole robot. Press Next to go step by step, and Back to return. Use the sliders and the Play button.

Key idea. A full turn is 2π, about 6.28 rad. A speed in rad/s says how many radians are turned every second. A wheel of radius W_r that turns at φ̇ rad/s moves along the floor at W_r × φ̇ metres per second.
06

The differential kinematic problem

A mobile robot has two wheels with radius r, and the distance between the wheels is L. The wheels are independently controlled, so the right wheel and the left wheel have their own speeds. The velocity of the robot in the world reference frame is Ṗ, made of an angular and a linear velocity. The problem is to find a function f that, from the radius of the wheels, their separation, the orientation θ of the robot and the speed of the wheels, gives the overall velocity of the robot in the two dimensional space. From Part 2 on, the radius is called W_r and the separation W_s.

Key idea. The differential kinematics is the function f from (L, r, θ, wheel speeds) to Ṗ. It is needed to send velocity commands to the robot and move it with a joystick.
07

Why the function f is still needed

The formula with two snapshots does give the velocity, but only after the robot has moved. The function f is different: it starts from the wheel speeds, which are the only thing that can be set on the robot. Press Play and watch when each way of getting the velocity becomes available.

Key idea. Snapshots measure the velocity after the move. The function f gives it from the wheel speeds, before the move, and works backwards too: from a wanted motion to the wheel speeds. That is what a joystick needs.
PART 2

Linear velocity

Whenever a problem is complex, it is broken into a series of smaller problems that can be solved easily. Here the first small problem is the equation that links the linear speed V of the robot to the speed of its wheels.

08

Breaking the problem into smaller ones

Assuming that the wheel radius, the separation of the wheels and the rotational speed of the wheels are known, the linear speed V and the angular speed ω of the robot are wanted. Rather than calculating everything at once, the work is split into smaller problems.

Key idea. A hard problem is split into small ones. First problem: the linear speed V from the wheels.
09

Three points of the robot and their speeds

To keep it simple, only the linear velocity at three notable points is considered: the centre of the robot C, the centre of the left wheel B and the centre of the right wheel A. The point C moves at the speed V, which is the speed of the robot. Two vectors connect B to C and A to C. The speeds of A and B come directly from the wheels.

Key idea. V_A = W_r φ̇R and V_B = W_r φ̇L. The speed V_C = V of the robot is what is missing.
10

The arrows r_CA and r_CB as plain numbers

The next sections use two arrows, r_CA and r_CB. Here they are turned into plain numbers on a ruler, so that adding and subtracting them becomes simple arithmetic. Move the slider to change the wheel separation. Press Next to go step by step, and Back to return.

Key idea. On a ruler with zero at C, r_CA = +W_s ÷ 2 and r_CB = −W_s ÷ 2. Added they give 0. Subtracted they give W_s.
11

From the speeds of A and B to the speed at C

V_C has two components: one depends on V_A and the other on V_B. Each one is the speed of a wheel point plus a turning part ω × r. Adding the two equations makes the turning parts disappear. Press Next to go step by step, and Back to return. Touch or hover a coloured part to see where it is in the picture.

Key idea. The vectors r_CA and r_CB are equal and opposite, so the turning parts cancel and V_C = (V_A + V_B) ÷ 2.
12

The equation for the linear speed

Putting V_A = W_r φ̇R and V_B = W_r φ̇L into the average gives the equation that links the linear velocity of the robot to the rotational velocity of the two wheels. This is equation one.

Key idea. V = W_r φ̇R ÷ 2 + W_r φ̇L ÷ 2. The linear speed is the average of the two wheel point speeds.
PART 3

Angular velocity

Now that the linear velocity is linked to the wheels, the angular velocity of the robot is linked to the wheels too. The same two equations are used, but this time they are subtracted.

13

Subtracting the two equations

To get the linear velocity, the two equations for the speed of the point C were added. To get the angular velocity, they are subtracted. Press Next to go step by step, and Back to return. Touch or hover a coloured part to see where it is in the picture.

Key idea. The angular speed is the difference of the two wheel point speeds divided by the wheel separation: ω = (V_A − V_B) ÷ W_s. This is equation two, and the reason for the name differential drive.
14

How the matrix is built from the two equations

Equation one and equation two are put in a table, step by step: first the number in front of each wheel speed, then the table itself, then how a row of the table gives back an equation, and finally the inverse table that goes backwards. Press Next to go step by step, and Back to return.

Key idea. The matrix M_s is just the numbers in front of the wheel speeds in the two equations. Row 1 is equation one and row 2 is equation two. Its inverse solves the same equations for the wheel speeds.
15

Both equations in one matrix

Equation one and equation two are joined in a matrix form. On one side are the linear and angular velocities of the robot, in the robot reference frame. On the other side are the rotational velocities of the wheels. The matrix that connects them is called M_s. Inverting it gives the opposite direction: from the velocity of the robot to the speeds of the wheels.

Key idea. One matrix M_s turns wheel speeds into (V, ω). Its inverse turns a wanted (V, ω) into wheel speeds. The inverse is used to control the robot with a joystick.
PART 4

Velocity in the world frame

V and ω live in the frame of the robot. The velocity wanted, Ṗ, lives in the world frame. A rotation matrix connects the two.

16

From the robot frame to the world frame

The velocity of the robot in the world frame is connected to the velocity in the robot frame by a rotation matrix that depends only on the orientation θ. Press Next to go step by step, and Back to return. Move the sliders and press the θ buttons to see how V is shared between ẋ and ẏ.

Key idea. Ṗ = R(θ) × (V, 0, ω). The matrix R(θ) turns the velocity of the robot frame into the velocity of the world frame. It only needs the angle θ.
PART 5

Differential forward kinematics

The problem was split in two parts so that each could be solved alone. Now the two equations are combined into the overall differential kinematics equation.

17

Putting the two equations together

The first equation connects the robot frame to the world frame with a matrix that only depends on θ. The second equation connects the linear and angular velocities of the robot to the rotational velocities of the wheels, with a matrix that only depends on the radius of the wheels and on their separation. The two matrices are multiplied. Press Next to go step by step.

Key idea. Multiplying R(θ) by M_s gives one matrix, the Jacobian. It links the rotational speeds of the wheels directly to the velocity of the robot in the world frame.
18

The function f at work

The equation found is the function f of the differential kinematic problem: it is based on the radius of the wheels, their distance, the orientation of the robot and the rotational velocities of the wheels, and it provides the overall velocity of the robot in the world reference frame.

Key idea. Ṗ = J(θ) × (φ̇R, φ̇L). From the wheels to the velocity of the robot in the world frame, in one equation.
PART 6

Simple speed controller

Now that the differential kinematics equation is known, it is used in code to move the robot with a joystick, with ROS 2.

19

From the joystick to the wheels

The joystick publishes velocity messages on a topic with the desired speed of the robot. It is more intuitive to command the robot in its own reference frame, for example to move straight at one metre per second or to rotate in place at one radian per second. But the two wheels have to be driven separately. So a new node subscribes to the velocity topic, applies the differential kinematics equation to each message, and publishes a command for each wheel on the topic that actuates the wheels.

Key idea. The node turns each velocity command from the joystick, in the robot frame, into the rotational speed of each wheel. It uses the inverse of the matrix M_s.
20

Summary and quiz

Key takeaways

  1. The pose P = (x, y, θ) gives the position and orientation. Two snapshots, P0 at t0 and P1 at t1, give a change. The change divided by the time is the velocity Ṗ = (ẋ, ẏ, θ̇).
  2. The differential kinematic problem is to find a function f from the wheel radius, the wheel separation, θ and the wheel speeds to Ṗ. It is needed to move the robot with a joystick.
  3. Three points are used: C (centre, speed V), A (right wheel) and B (left wheel). V_A = W_r φ̇R and V_B = W_r φ̇L.
  4. Adding V_C = V_A + ω × r_CA and V_C = V_B + ω × r_CB makes the vectors cancel. V = W_r φ̇R ÷ 2 + W_r φ̇L ÷ 2. This is equation one.
  5. Subtracting them gives ω = (V_A − V_B) ÷ W_s, that is ω = W_r φ̇R ÷ W_s − W_r φ̇L ÷ W_s. This is equation two. It depends on the difference of the wheel speeds, which gives the name differential drive.
  6. Both equations form the matrix M_s: (V, ω) = M_s × (φ̇R, φ̇L). Inverting M_s gives the wheel speeds from V and ω.
  7. The velocity in the world frame is Ṗ = R(θ) × (V, 0, ω). The 0 is there because the robot cannot move sideways. ẋ = V cos θ, ẏ = V sin θ, θ̇ = ω.
  8. Multiplying R(θ) by M_s gives the Jacobian. Ṗ = J(θ) × (φ̇R, φ̇L) is the forward differential kinematics, the function f that was wanted.
  9. A simple speed controller node receives (V, ω) from /cmd_vel, uses the inverse of M_s, and sends a speed command to each wheel.

The translational part of the pose

Where is the robot, and where is the obstacle it sees?

This chapter studies only the translational part of the motion: how to write the X and Y position of the robot in the world, with the turning set aside. A vector T joins the centre of the world frame to the centre of the robot frame. The same vector then turns out to be very useful: it brings what a sensor sees, relative to the robot, into the world. Every step has a simulator: a robot in the world, a vector with its two projections, a sensor that only knows relative numbers, an addition of two arrows, and a map that builds up as the robot drives. All numbers on the page are teaching values.

01

The simplified problem

The study of the motion starts with its translational part, and with how to write the X and Y coordinates of the pose of the robot. The simplified problem is to find the position of the robot frame R with respect to the fixed world frame W. The rotational part of the motion is assumed to be zero, so the frame R is always oriented like the frame W.

Key idea. With no rotation, R keeps the orientation of W, and only the position of R is left to describe.
02

The translation vector and its components

To describe the two translational components X and Y, a vector T is drawn that connects the centre of W with the centre of R. Projecting it on the x axis and on the y axis of W gives two components: x and y. They are the two components of the position of R in W. The two equations can also be written in matrix form with the same meaning. With real numbers, a frame R at coordinates 3 and 2 sits at 3 along x and 2 along y.

Key idea. The vector T from the centre of W to the centre of R, projected on the axes, gives x and y: the position of R in W.
03

A sensor measures relative to the robot

Say the robot has an onboard sensor that detects obstacles around it. Such a sensor also gives relative information about the position of each obstacle, relative to the sensor itself. For example: “an obstacle is two metres away from the robot”. That is not enough to know where the obstacle is globally, so the position of the obstacle with respect to the frame W is unknown.

Key idea. Sensor data is relative to the robot. It does not say where the obstacle is in the world.
04

Bringing the obstacle into the world

The problem can be written like this: the position of the obstacle P in the robot frame R is known, with coordinates xp and yp. The position of the frame R in the world is known too. Where is the obstacle P in the frame W? It is intuitive and needs only an addition: the position of P in W is the position of P in R plus the position of R itself with respect to W, which is the vector T. In matrix form, each vector is split into its x and y components.

Key idea. P in W = P in R + T, written PW = T + PR. Adding the translation vector brings a relative measurement into the world frame.
05

The translation vector at work

Whenever a sensor on the robot gives a measurement to a new obstacle that is relative to the robot, it can be brought into the world frame simply by adding the translation vector, and the position of the obstacle in the world follows automatically. The translation vector T represents the distance between the centre of the frame R and the centre of the frame W. Watch it build a map.

Key idea. Each new relative reading plus T gives the obstacle in the world. T is called the translation vector.
06

Summary and quiz

Key takeaways

  1. With the rotation set to zero, R keeps the orientation of W.
  2. The vector T joins the centre of W to the centre of R. Its projections on the axes are x and y, the position of R in W.
  3. The two equations can be written in matrix form. The meaning stays the same.
  4. Sensor measurements are relative to the robot, so they do not give a world position.
  5. P in W = P in R + T, written PW = T + PR.
  6. T is called the translation vector. It is the distance between the centres of R and W.

The key equation

P in W=P in R+T

Add the translation vector to a relative reading, and the point is in the world frame.

The general case: moved and turned

Where is the obstacle in the world, when the robot is both moved and turned?

The earlier chapters described the two parts of the pose separately: the translation, which says where the robot is, and the rotation, which says which way it faces. This chapter puts them together. The robot frame R is moved by a vector T and turned by an angle θ with respect to the world frame W. A sensor gives the place of an obstacle in R, and the place in W is needed. The answer is one matrix, the transformation matrix, which is a rotation matrix and a translation vector put together. Every step has a simulator: the pose with its three numbers, the sensor reading, the two moves one at a time, the matrix forms, the worked example, and a driving robot. All numbers on the page are teaching values.

Words used on this page (open this if a word is new)
translationA move. It changes where an object is, not which way it faces.
translation vector TThe arrow from the centre of the world frame W to the centre of the robot frame R. It has two numbers: Tx to the right and Ty up.
rotationA turn. It changes the direction an object faces, not its position.
angle θ (theta)The turn between the x axis of W and the x axis of R. Counter-clockwise is positive.
posePlace and direction together. In two dimensions the pose is three numbers: Tx, Ty and θ.
frame WThe fixed world frame. It is stuck to the floor and never moves.
frame RThe frame attached to the robot. It moves and turns with the robot.
sensor readingWhat the robot sensor reports: the place of an obstacle measured from the robot, along x_R and y_R.
P in RThe two numbers xP and yP that say where the obstacle P is, measured along the axes of the robot frame R.
P in WThe two numbers xPW and yPW that say where the same obstacle P is, measured along the axes of the world frame W. This is what a map needs.
cosine and sineTwo values from a calculator for an angle. For 30 degrees they are about 0.87 and 0.5. Chapter 37 explained where they come from. This page only uses them.
matrixA block of numbers in rows and columns. Multiplying it by a column of numbers gives a new column.
rotation matrixThe block of four numbers (cosθ, minus sinθ, sinθ, cosθ). It turns a reading from R into the directions of W.
transformation matrixThe square matrix that holds a rotation matrix and a translation vector together. One multiplication turns a reading and adds T.
homogeneous formThe way of writing the transformation matrix with an extra row 0, 0, 1 and an extra 1 under the reading. It adds no new information. It only makes the matrix square.
two movesA way to see the maths: first turn the reading as if the centre of R were on the centre of W, then slide by T.

01

The robot frame is moved and turned

Two things have been seen so far. The position of a body in two dimensions can be given by a translation vector, and its orientation by a rotation matrix. Now both are combined to describe the general case of a robot moving in two dimensions. In the general case, the reference frame R attached to the robot is both moved and turned with respect to the world frame W. The vector T gives the translation between the two frames, and the angle θ gives the relative orientation.

Key idea. The pose of the robot in two dimensions is three numbers: the translation T (Tx and Ty) and the angle θ.
02

The sensor gives the obstacle in the robot frame

Assume that the robot has a sensor that can detect an obstacle, and that the sensor gives the position of the obstacle in the robot reference frame. Knowing this, the goal is to calculate the position of the obstacle, the point P, in the world reference frame. The known values are the vector T, the angle θ and the reading of the sensor. The unknown values are the two world coordinates of P.

Key idea. The sensor reading is measured along the moved and turned axes of R. The map needs numbers along the straight axes of W. The task is to convert one into the other.
03

Turn first, then slide: the two equations combined

The translation equation and the rotation equation were solved separately in the earlier chapters. Combining them gives one calculation. Here it is built in small steps, one Next press at a time: first the turn, as if the centre of R sat on the centre of W, then the slide by T. Touch or hover a coloured number to see where it is in the picture.

Key idea. P in W = (turned reading) + T. The turn uses the rotation matrix and the angle. The slide adds Tx and Ty.
04

From two equations to one matrix

Combining the translation equation and the rotation equation gives a new matrix that holds the rotation and the translation together. It gives the position of P in W as a function of the position of P in R. Very often this matrix is written in a homogeneous form: a new row is added. That row does not contain any useful information. It only makes the matrix square, and that makes the matrix operations easier. In this form the matrix is called the transformation matrix: the composition of a rotation matrix and a translation vector.

Key idea. The transformation matrix is a rotation matrix and a translation vector in one square block. Multiplied by the reading (with a 1 added), it gives the position in W.
05

The numerical example

Assume that the coordinates of P in the robot frame are known, and also the orientation θ and the translation between the frames. Substituting these values into the transformation matrix leaves only the coordinates of P in the world frame as unknowns. Solving the matrix product gives them. The example: θ = 30°, T = (3, 2) and P in R = (2, 1). It is worked one row at a time.

Key idea. Row 1 gives the x of P in W (4.23) and row 2 gives the y (3.87). Row 3 only gives back the 1.
06

A driving robot and fixed obstacles

Now the robot can say where an obstacle is in the world, just by knowing its own position and orientation and the position of the obstacle relative to the robot. The obstacles are fixed. The robot drives and turns, so every reading changes, and the transformation matrix brings each one back to the same world position. The other two buttons show what goes wrong when the translation or the rotation is forgotten.

Key idea. The readings change with every move and turn. The result in W does not, because the transformation matrix removes the move and the turn.
07

Summary and quiz

Key takeaways

  1. In the general case the robot frame R is moved by the vector T and turned by the angle θ with respect to the world frame W. The pose is Tx, Ty and θ.
  2. A sensor gives the place of an obstacle P in R. The place of P in W is needed.
  3. Combining the translation equation and the rotation equation gives one matrix. It does two moves: turn the reading, then add T.
  4. In homogeneous form a row 0, 0, 1 is added, and a 1 under the reading. This only makes the matrix square.
  5. The square matrix, a rotation matrix and a translation vector together, is the transformation matrix.
  6. Example: θ = 30°, T = (3, 2), P in R = (2, 1) gives P in W = (4.23, 3.87).

The key equation

P in W=transformation matrix×P in R

Multiply a relative reading by the transformation matrix, and the numbers are measured from W instead of R, whatever the position and direction of the robot.

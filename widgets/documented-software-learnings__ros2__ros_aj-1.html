The rotational part of the pose

Which way does the robot face, and how does that change what it sees?

The second basic part of the pose in two dimensions is the rotation: how the robot is oriented in the world. This chapter describes it with one angle, θ, between the world frame W and the robot frame R. The translation is set to zero so that the centres of the two frames are the same point. The chapter then solves one problem: a sensor gives the place of an obstacle in the turned robot frame, and the place in the world frame is needed. The answer is a pair of equations, which can also be written with the rotation matrix. A 47-step lesson builds cos and sin from scratch, starting with Pythagoras. Every step has a simulator: an angle to turn, one point with two pairs of numbers, arrows projected on the axes, the matrix with its numbers, a worked example, and a robot that turns while the obstacles stay. All numbers on the page are teaching values.

Words used on this page (open this if a word is new)
rotationA turn. It changes the direction an object faces and does not change its position.
orientationThe direction an object faces. For the robot it says which way its front points in the world.
frame WThe fixed world frame. It is stuck to the floor and never moves.
frame RThe frame attached to the robot. It turns with the robot.
angle θ (theta)The turn between the x axis of W and the x axis of R. Counter-clockwise is positive.
degrees and radiansTwo ways to measure a turn. A full turn is 360 degrees, or about 6.28 radians. The simulators use degrees.
coordinatesThe numbers that say where a point is, such as 2 along x and 3 along y.
projectionThe shadow of a point or an arrow on an axis. It gives one coordinate.
rotated frameA frame with the same centre as W, but turned by the angle θ. Its axes are called x′ and y′, or x_R and y_R.
Px, PyThe two coordinates of the point P in the world frame W.
Px′, Py′The two coordinates of the same point P in the rotated frame R. The small mark ′ is read “prime” or “first”.
cosine and sineTwo numbers between 0 and 1 that only depend on an angle. For an arrow that leans by the angle, cos gives the shadow next to the angle and sin the shadow across from it, as fractions of the arrow length. For 0 degrees: cos 1, sin 0. For 90 degrees: cos 0, sin 1. For 30 degrees: about 0.87 and 0.5.
shadowAnother word for projection: where an arrow lands on an axis when light shines straight onto that axis.
matrixA block of numbers in rows and columns. Multiplying it by a column of numbers gives a new column.
rotation matrixThe block of four numbers (cos, −sin, sin, cos) that turns coordinates from the rotated frame into world coordinates. It only contains θ.
sensorA part that lets the robot measure the world, for example one that finds obstacles.
P in R, P in WThe same point P, written with numbers measured from the robot frame, and written with numbers measured from the world frame.
right triangleA triangle with one square corner (90°). The two sides that touch the corner are the legs. The slanted side opposite the corner is the hypotenuse.
Pythagoras formulaIn a right triangle, leg² + leg² = hypotenuse². For legs 3 and 1 the slanted side is √10, about 3.16.

01

Orientation and the angle θ

The second basic component that defines the pose of an object in two dimensions is the rotation, and so how the object is oriented in the world. The problem is to specify the orientation of the robot frame R with respect to the world frame W. To keep the calculation simple, the translational component is assumed to be zero, so the centre of R matches the centre of W. The orientation of R is described by one number, the angle θ, the displacement angle between the two frames. To calculate it, some trigonometry is needed.

Key idea. The orientation of R with respect to W is one number: the angle θ between the two frames. The centres are the same point.
02

The same point in two frames

Start with the fixed frame W and assume that there is an obstacle P in the world. Projecting P on the x axis and the y axis of W gives its coordinates Px and Py. In two dimensions these two numbers are enough to say where P is. Now draw a new frame with the same origin as W, but rotated: the displacement between the two is the angle θ, and its axes are called x′ and y′. Projecting P on these axes gives Px′ and Py′, the position of P in the rotated frame.

Key idea. One point has two pairs of coordinates: (Px, Py) in the world frame and (Px′, Py′) in the rotated frame. Only the frame it is measured from differs.
03

The maths first: Pythagoras, cos and sin from scratch

The next section needs cos and sin, so they are built here from nothing in 47 small steps. The lesson starts with the right triangle and the Pythagoras formula. It shows that cos and sin are only the shadow and the height of a stick of length 1, finds real numbers for 30°, 45° and 60°, and works out sin 17° and cos 17° as a worked example. At the end everything is used for the robot turned by 30°, which gives the rotation matrix. Press Next to go on and Back to go back. Drag the angle slider when it appears.

Key idea. cos θ is the shadow and sin θ is the height of a stick of length 1 leaning at the angle θ. Pythagoras links them: cos²θ + sin²θ = 1. With them, 3 steps forward and 1 step left become (2.1, 2.37) in world numbers for a robot turned by 30°.
04

From the rotated frame to the world frame

Now the problem can be stated. The sensor of the robot gives Px′ and Py′, the place of P measured along the tilted axes of R. The angle θ between R and W is known. How can Px and Py, the place of P along the straight axes of W, be calculated? This is the part that uses cos and sin, so it is split into 15 tiny steps. Press Next to move on and Back to return. Nothing is assumed: cos and sin are explained from the start, and the reason for every multiplication and for the minus sign is shown.

Key idea. Px = Px′ cosθ − Py′ sinθ and Py = Px′ sinθ + Py′ cosθ. Each term is one of the parts found in steps 7 to 10 of the lesson above.
05

The rotation matrix

The two equations can also be written in matrix form without changing their meaning. In this form a relation is clear: the coordinates of P in W and the coordinates of P in the rotated frame are linked by a matrix that depends only on the displacement angle θ between the two frames. This matrix is called the rotation matrix.

Key idea. The rotation matrix contains only θ. Multiplied by the coordinates in the rotated frame, it gives the coordinates in the world frame.
06

The robot example with numbers

Back to the robot. Assume that the robot detects an obstacle with its onboard sensors and gives its position in the robot frame R, which is rotated with respect to W. The question is unchanged: knowing xP and yP in R and the angle θ, what are the coordinates of P in W? The equation that relates the two frames through the rotation matrix was just derived. With real values for xP, yP and θ, the only two unknowns are the coordinates of P in the world, and expanding the product of the matrix and the column gives them.

Key idea. With the numbers put in, the matrix equation has only the two world coordinates unknown, and expanding it gives them.
07

The robot turns, the obstacles stay

The same idea works for every reading. When the robot turns in place, the obstacles do not move, but the numbers from the sensor change, because they are relative to the turned frame. Turning each reading with the rotation matrix gives back the same world position every time.

Key idea. Readings in R change as the robot turns. The rotation matrix brings each one to the same place in W.
08

Summary and quiz

Key takeaways

  1. The rotation says how the robot is oriented in the world. It is described by one angle, θ, between R and W.
  2. With the translation set to zero, the centres of R and W are the same point.
  3. A point P has coordinates (Px, Py) in W and (Px′, Py′) in the rotated frame.
  4. Projecting the vectors Px′ and Py′ on the axes of W gives Px = Px′ cosθ − Py′ sinθ and Py = Px′ sinθ + Py′ cosθ.
  5. In matrix form, the block of cos and sin is the rotation matrix. It depends only on θ.
  6. A sensor reading in R and the angle θ give the position in W by multiplying with the rotation matrix.

The key equation

P in W=rotation matrix×P in R

Multiply a relative reading by the rotation matrix, and the numbers are measured from W instead of R (with the two centres at the same point).

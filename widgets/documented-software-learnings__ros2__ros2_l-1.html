Rolling friction and wheeled efficiency

Why wheels waste energy, and why sand is the worst

A wheel is one of the best ways to move a robot, but only on some ground. On a smooth floor a robot rolls far on a small battery. On sand the same robot stops almost at once. The reason is that wheels and ground squash a little, and squashing costs energy. This page follows that idea step by step with a simulator at every step: a race on four floors, a perfect wheel, a squashing wheel, a wheel made of springs, spring energy graphs, rolling friction and a wheel in sand. Everything here is a simulation, so no robot is needed.

Words used on this page (open this if a word is new)
force, N (newton)A push or a pull. N is the unit for force. A 1 kg object resting on the ground pushes down with about 10 N. A 100 kg robot pushes down with about 981 N.
rigidDoes not squash or bend at all, like a perfect steel block.
deformationA change of shape because of a push. Squashing is a deformation.
permanent deformationA change of shape that stays after the push is gone, like a footprint in sand.
contact point PThe single spot where a perfectly rigid wheel touches the ground.
contact area (surface S)The patch where a real, squashing wheel touches the ground. It has a size, so it is not a point.
ω (omega), angular velocityHow fast the wheel turns around its middle.
energy, J (joule)The ability to do work. A battery stores energy. J is the unit. Lifting a 1 kg object by about 10 cm takes about 1 J.
workEnergy moved by a force over a distance. Pushing a spring in is work done on the spring.
springSomething that squeezes in when pushed and springs back out when let go.
compression, decompressionCompression means squeezing in. Decompression means springing back out.
stiffness, kHow hard a spring is to squeeze. A bigger k means a stiffer spring.
Hooke’s law, F = k × xFor an ideal spring, the force F is the stiffness k times the squeeze x. Twice the force gives twice the squeeze.
energy loss, dissipatedEnergy that turns into heat or into a lasting change of shape, instead of moving the robot.
rolling frictionThe energy lost to squashing and springing back as a wheel rolls. It slows the wheel down.
efficiencyHow much of the energy goes into useful motion. High efficiency means little is wasted.

01

Why is wheeled movement sometimes inefficient?

Wheeled movement is very efficient on some ground and very inefficient on other ground. Hard and flat ground, like a smooth floor, asphalt or concrete, is good. Soft or uneven ground, like sand, mud, grass or loose soil, is bad. Here is the same robot with the same battery on four different floors. Press the start button and watch which one gets furthest.

Key idea. Question: why does this happen? The main reason is deformation, the squashing of the wheel, the ground, or both. More squashing means more energy loss, and that means lower efficiency.
02

The perfect wheel on a perfect surface

To see what goes wrong in real life, first look at a perfect case. Take a rigid wheel, which never squashes, on rigid ground, which never squashes either. A steel train wheel on a steel rail is a good example. Because nothing squashes, the wheel and the ground touch at one single point, called P. A force F pushes the wheel, and the wheel turns with angular velocity ω. Now imagine this happening in a perfect vacuum with no energy loss at all. After one push, the wheel would roll for ever.

Key idea. Rigid wheel and rigid ground means no squashing, one contact point P, and no energy loss. This is the ideal case. Real wheels and real ground always squash a little.
03

Real wheels squash, so contact is an area

In the real world, wheels and ground are not perfectly rigid. At the place where they touch, the wheel squashes and often the ground does too. So the contact is no longer one point. It is a patch with a size, called the contact surface S. How much squashing happens depends on three things: the weight of the vehicle, the material of the wheel (softer rubber squashes more than steel) and the material of the ground (soft or uneven ground squashes more). A steel wheel on a steel rail squashes very little. A rubber wheel on a heavily loaded truck squashes much more. Even when the squashing is very small, it still exists.

Key idea. Real wheels have a contact surface S, not an ideal point P. More weight, a softer wheel or softer ground makes more squashing.
04

The wheel as many small springs

A helpful way to think about the squashing is to imagine the wheel is made of many small springs. When one part of the wheel reaches the ground, its springs are squeezed. This is compression. As the wheel turns, that part leaves the ground, and its springs spring back out to normal. This is decompression. So every bit of the wheel goes through the same cycle again and again: compression, then decompression.

Key idea. A rolling wheel acts like many small springs that are squeezed and released over and over. The next sections show what that costs in energy.
05

Springs store energy: compression and decompression

To understand what happens in the wheel, first look at one simple spring. Push on it with a force F and it squeezes by a distance x. The bigger the force, the bigger the squeeze, in a straight-line way: F = k × x. This is Hooke’s law. If the force and the squeeze are drawn as a graph, the area under the line is the work (energy) used to squeeze the spring. That energy is stored in the spring. When the force is removed, the spring springs back and gives the energy back. For an ideal spring, the energy put in equals the energy recovered, so an ideal spring has no energy loss.

Key idea. Squeezing a spring stores energy. Letting go gives it back. The area under the force against squeeze graph shows that energy.
06

A real spring loses some energy

The real world is different. A real spring does not give back all the energy that went in. Squeezing and letting go follow two different lines on the graph. At first a small force squeezes the spring, and later much more force is needed for a little extra squeeze. On the way back out, the spring pushes with less force than it took to squeeze it. The area under the pressing line is the work needed to squeeze the spring. The area between the two lines is the energy that was lost, mostly as heat inside the spring, and sometimes as a lasting change of shape. So for a real spring, the energy used to compress it is more than the energy recovered.

Key idea. In a real spring, energy used to compress is greater than energy recovered. The gap between the two lines is the energy lost.
07

Rolling friction: the energy lost by a rolling wheel

A real wheel is like a real spring. Every turn, the part at the ground is squeezed and then springs back, and not all of the energy comes back. The energy lost in this squeezing and springing back is called rolling friction. The bigger the energy loss, the bigger the rolling friction, and the lower the efficiency. So the key chain is: more deformation, more energy loss, more rolling friction, lower efficiency. The boxes below follow that chain with numbers. Key idea. More deformation leads to more energy loss, then more rolling friction, then lower efficiency. This energy loss is the important idea for wheels.
08

Soft ground: the sand keeps its dent

Now take a wheel on soft ground like sand. Here both the wheel and the ground squash, so both of them lose energy. The wheel is still a set of springs that squeeze and spring back. The ground can be pictured as many small springs too, with one big difference. A wheel spring goes compression, then decompression. A sand spring goes compression, then permanent deformation. The sand does not return to its old shape, which is why a wheel leaves tracks. The energy used to squash the sand is not recovered. It goes into making a lasting dent in the ground.

Key idea. On sand, the wheel squashes and the ground squashes for good. The energy used to dent the ground is not given back. This is why wheeled locomotion is very inefficient on soft ground.
09

Hard ground against soft ground, side by side

Here is the final explanation in one picture. On hard ground, only the wheel squashes a little, so some energy is lost and efficiency is relatively high. On soft ground, the wheel squashes and the ground squashes too. That is a much bigger loss, and because the ground stays dented, a large amount of energy ends up inside the ground. Change the wheel and the weight to see how the two parts of the loss change.

Key idea. Hard surface: wheel squashing only, some loss. Soft surface: wheel squashing plus ground squashing, a much greater loss. Wheeled locomotion is much less efficient on soft ground such as sand.
10

Summary and quiz

Must learn

  1. Real wheels deform (squash) while moving.
  2. Real squashing causes energy loss.
  3. This energy loss adds up to rolling friction.
  4. Hard surfaces squash less, so the energy loss is lower.
  5. Soft surfaces such as sand squash a lot.
  6. On sand, both the wheel and the ground deform.
  7. Sand deforms permanently.
  8. The energy used to deform the sand is not recovered.
  9. So wheeled movement becomes very inefficient on soft ground.

Good to know

  • An ideal rigid wheel on rigid ground touches at a single point.
  • Real wheels touch with a surface, not an ideal point.
  • Steel wheels squash much less than rubber wheels under heavy loads.
  • The area between the compression and decompression lines is the energy lost.
  • The spring model is a simple way to see squashing and energy loss.

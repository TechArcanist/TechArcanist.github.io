Chapter 72: sensor fusion

Advantages of Having Multiple Sensors Why one sensor is never enough, and how combining several gives a better answer

The last chapters showed that every sensor has errors, and that a robot works with probabilities because of them. This page asks the next question: what if the robot has more than one sensor? Each one is good in some conditions and useless in others, just like the human senses. Combining them, called sensor fusion, gives an answer better than any single sensor. Every number on this page is an example chosen to teach, and it says so.

Words used on this page (open this if a word is new)

cameraSees features of the surroundings, used for localization and to avoid obstacles.
laser sensorScans around the robot and measures the distance to nearby obstacles, usually very accurately.
GPSGives a position using signals from satellites.
slippageA wheel that turns without moving the robot the same distance.
satellite coverageHow well the satellite signals reach the robot.
error (uncertainty)How far a sensor reading can be from the truth. A big error means a lot of uncertainty.
weightHow much the fusion trusts one sensor. A smaller error gives a bigger weight.
fused estimateThe combined answer of all the sensors.

The human body as a sensor system

A person has five senses and a nervous system that links them. Each sense works well in some situations and badly in others, and the brain decides which one to trust. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Each sensor is suitable and accurate for a specific purpose under specific conditions. Smell notices a pizza before the eyes see the restaurant, hearing recognises a voice before the face is seen, and sight is useless in the dark. The brain is the best sensor fusion system: it combines many signals and decides in real time which to rely on.

The sensors of a robot

A robot has its own senses: pieces of silicon and metal that measure something. Each one measures a different thing. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Encoder: how far the wheels turned. Gyroscope: how the robot is turned (orientation). Accelerometer: how fast the speed changes, from which speed and position can be worked out. Camera: features of the surroundings. Laser: distances to nearby obstacles. GPS: position from satellites. A robot normally has only the sensors its task needs.

03

A good sensor in one place is useless in another

Wheel encoders are fine on a smooth floor and almost useless in mud. GPS is fine outdoors and useless inside a closed warehouse. The conditions decide. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Encoders are almost useless off road, where the wheels slip and the turns of the wheel no longer match the distance moved. GPS is useless in a closed warehouse because there is not enough satellite coverage. Indoors the encoders and the laser are better, with the laser accurate to centimetres. No single sensor is best everywhere.

04

Sensor fusion: two sensors, one better answer

Sensor fusion receives several signals, filters them and combines them into one signal that is an improved version of all of them. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Sensor fusion combines the signals of several sensors, with their uncertainty, into one estimate that is better than each of them: closer to the truth and less uncertain. This makes the localization of the robot more robust and reliable, and notably reduces errors.

05

The fusion brain: who to trust now

A good fusion system, like the brain, changes how much it relies on each sensor when the conditions change. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. The weight of a sensor depends on how reliable it is in the current conditions: a smaller error gives a bigger weight. When the robot goes from a warehouse to an off-road path, the weight moves from the laser and the encoders to the other sensors, and the fused answer stays good.

Summary and quiz

Key takeaways

  1. Each sensor is suitable for a specific purpose under specific conditions. The human body shows it: smell finds a pizza early, hearing recognises a voice, sight is useless in the dark.
  2. The brain is the best sensor fusion system: it processes many signals and decides in real time which ones to rely on.
  3. A robot uses encoders (wheel turns), gyroscopes (orientation), accelerometers (acceleration), cameras (features), lasers (distances) and GPS (satellite position). It only has the sensors its task needs.
  4. Encoders are almost useless off road because of wheel slippage. GPS is useless in a closed warehouse. Indoors, encoders and the laser are the better choice.
  5. Sensor fusion combines several signals into one that is better than each: more robust, more reliable, with smaller errors.
  6. With two sensors, one at 10.0 m with an error of 0.8 m and one at 10.3 m with an error of 0.3 m, the fused answer is 10.26 m with an error of 0.28 m (example numbers).
  7. A good fusion relies more on the sensors that are reliable in the current conditions, and that changes when the conditions change.

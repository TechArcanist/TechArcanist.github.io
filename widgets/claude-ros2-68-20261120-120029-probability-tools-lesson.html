Chapter 68: probability tools

Probability Tools for Localization Random variables, distributions, the bell curve and Bayes rule, built from dice and coins

Chapter 67 showed why a robot is never sure where it is and gave every place a share between 0 and 1. This page adds the tools needed to work with those shares: random variables, combining two events, the mean and the spread, the bell curve, the total probability rule and Bayes rule. Every symbol is explained in words before it is used, and every formula is shown with real numbers. The last section explains why real sensors are noisy.

Words used on this page (open this if a word is new)

likelihoodHow likely the reading is if the guess were true.

Random variables

Two experiments: independent and dependent events

A coin and a die do not affect each other. A robot position and a rock detector do. The way to combine their probabilities is different. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Independent events: P(both) = P(first) × P(second). Dependent events: P(X and Y) = P(X) × P(Y given X). Turned around: P(Y given X) = P(X and Y) ÷ P(X). The bar | means given, and ∩ means and.

03

Probability distributions

A probability distribution lists the probability of every possible result. Its shape shows at a glance which results are likely. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. A distribution gives the probability of every value of a random variable. One die gives a flat (uniform) distribution, 1 ÷ 6 for each face. The sum of two dice is not flat: 7 has 6 ways and 2 has 1 way, so 7 is six times as likely. A uniform distribution is the natural starting guess when nothing is known.

04

The mean and the spread

Two numbers describe a distribution: where its most likely value is, and how far the values are spread around it. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Mean = add up (value × probability). Variance = add up ((value − mean)² × probability). Standard deviation σ = the square root of the variance. For the sum of two dice: mean 7, variance 5.83, σ 2.4. A big σ means a lot of uncertainty.

05

The Gaussian: the bell curve

Add up many random pieces and the shape of the result becomes a smooth bell. This curve, the Gaussian or normal distribution, is described by only two numbers. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. A Gaussian is fixed by its mean μ (the middle, the most likely value) and its standard deviation σ (the spread). A tall thin bell means little uncertainty, a low wide bell a lot. The area under the whole curve is 1.

06

Combining Gaussians

Gaussians are easy to combine. Two curves multiplied give another Gaussian, and two curves added give another Gaussian, each with simple formulas for the new mean and spread. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Product: μ = (σB² × μA + σA² × μB) ÷ (σA² + σB²) and 1 ÷ σ² = 1 ÷ σA² + 1 ÷ σB², so the result is narrower than both. Sum: μ = μA + μB and σ² = σA² + σB², so the result is wider than both.

07

The total probability rule

Sometimes the probability of an event is hard to find directly but easy to find piece by piece. Split the world into regions, work out each piece, and add. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. P(A) = P(A given B1) × P(B1) + P(A given B2) × P(B2) + P(A given B3) × P(B3). The B regions must cover everything without overlapping, so their probabilities add up to 1.

08

Bayes rule: update with new evidence

After a new reading, how should an old guess change? Bayes rule is the exact recipe, and it comes from the product rule of section 2. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. P(A given B) = P(B given A) × P(A) ÷ P(B): posterior = likelihood × prior ÷ marginal. The posterior of one reading becomes the prior of the next, so the guess improves with every reading.

Sensor noise

Why is any of this needed? Real sensors are not perfect, and the encoders in the simulation have been perfect so far. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Noise is an unwanted random fluctuation added to a sensor signal. It comes from the sensor itself (such as temperature) and from the cables and electronics. A noisy reading is the clean value plus a random number, which is why a probabilistic approach is needed.

10

Summary and quiz

Key takeaways

  1. A random variable is the set of possible results of a random experiment. Each value has a probability from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain), and all of them add up to 1.
  2. Loaded coin: if heads has probability 0.6, tails has 1 − 0.6 = 0.4.
  3. Independent events multiply: heads and a 2 on a die is 1 ÷ 2 × 1 ÷ 6 = 1 ÷ 12.
  4. Dependent events: P(X and Y) = P(X) × P(Y given X). With P(A) = 0.5 and P(rock detected given A) = 0.8, both together is 0.4.
  5. A distribution gives the probability of every value. One die is flat (uniform); the sum of two dice has its peak at 7, six times as likely as 2.
  6. Mean = add up value × probability (7 for two dice). Variance = add up (value − mean)² × probability (5.83). Standard deviation = √variance (2.4).
  7. A Gaussian is fixed by its mean and standard deviation, and the area under it is 1. A tall thin bell means little uncertainty.
  8. Multiplying two Gaussians gives a narrower Gaussian; adding two gives a wider one. Both have simple formulas for the new mean and σ.
  9. Total probability: P(A) = P(A given B1) × P(B1) + P(A given B2) × P(B2) + P(A given B3) × P(B3).
  10. Bayes rule: P(A given B) = P(B given A) × P(A) ÷ P(B). With prior 0.5, likelihood 0.8 and marginal 0.5 the posterior is 0.8.
  11. Real sensors have noise: the clean value plus a random number. The next lab adds a random process to the simulated encoder to make it realistic.

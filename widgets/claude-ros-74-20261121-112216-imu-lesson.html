Chapter 74: the accelerometer and the IMU

Accelerometer and IMU How a mass on a spring tells a robot how hard it speeds up, and how one chip carries both sensors

Chapter 73 gave the robot a gyroscope for its orientation. This page adds a second sensor, the accelerometer, which measures the acceleration applied to the robot and so helps to estimate its speed. The physics starts from zero: a mass on a spring, the push needed to accelerate a mass, how the squeeze of the spring gives the acceleration, how three of these fit in a chip, and why the gyroscope and the accelerometer sit together in one unit called an IMU. Every number is an example, built step by step.

Why add an accelerometer

The gyroscope tells how the robot turns. A second sensor can tell how hard the robot is being sped up, and that helps to estimate its speed. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. An accelerometer measures the acceleration applied to the robot. Adding it up over time gives the speed: speed = sum of (acceleration × time step). Starting from rest, a constant acceleration a for a time t gives v = a × t.

02

A mass and a spring

An accelerometer can be simplified to a small mass joined to a spring. Two laws of physics are needed: how hard a mass has to be pushed to accelerate, and how a spring pushes back when it is squeezed. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Newton's law: force F = mass m × acceleration a. The spring law: force F = k × x, where k is the elastic constant of the spring and x is how much it is squeezed.
Related: Chapter 73: springs, F = k × x

03

From the squeeze to the acceleration

Put the mass and the spring in a case and speed the case up. The mass lags behind and squeezes the spring. The two laws of section 2 meet here. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. The force that accelerates the mass is the spring force, so m × a = k × x. Dividing by m gives a = k × x ÷ m. The mass m and the constant k are known from how the sensor is built, so measuring the squeeze x gives the acceleration.

04

Measuring the squeeze

The squeeze is tiny, a few millimetres or less. The chip has to turn it into a number. The choice of spring decides what the sensor can measure. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. The sensor measures the squeeze x in some way and calculates a = k × x ÷ m. A soft spring squeezes more for the same acceleration (more sensitive) but runs out of room sooner; a stiff spring measures bigger accelerations with a smaller squeeze.

05

Three axes and the IMU

Modern accelerometers fit three such sensors in a chip about the size of a coin, one for each direction. Together with a gyroscope the chip is called an IMU. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Three accelerometers measure the acceleration along x, y and z. An IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit) puts a gyroscope (roll, pitch and yaw) and an accelerometer (x, y and z) in a single chip: six numbers.

06

Strengths and limits

The accelerometer reacts at once to every speed change and does not look at the wheels. But the speed comes from adding up, and adding up has a price. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. A small constant error (bias) in the acceleration is added up into the speed (error = bias × time) and added up again into the distance (error = ½ × bias × time²). A tilt makes the sensor feel part of gravity as a false acceleration. Using the IMU together with the wheel encoders keeps the errors in check.
Related: Chapter 73: gyroscope drift, Chapter 72: sensor fusion

07

Summary and quiz

Key takeaways

  1. An accelerometer measures the acceleration applied to the robot, and adding it up gives the speed, for example 2 m/s² for 4 s gives 8 m/s.
  2. It works like a mass joined to a spring: force F = m × a for the mass, force F = k × x for the spring.
  3. Both forces are the same push, so m × a = k × x and a = k × x ÷ m. Measure the squeeze x and the acceleration follows, for example 3 mm with k = 20 N/m and m = 10 g gives 6 m/s².
  4. A soft spring is more sensitive but has a smaller range; a stiff spring has a bigger range and is less sensitive.
  5. A chip the size of a coin holds three such sensors for x, y and z. An IMU adds a gyroscope to it: roll, pitch and yaw plus x, y and z.
  6. Adding up a constant error grows it: a bias of 0.05 m/s² gives 3 m/s of speed error after one minute and 90 m of distance error.
  7. A tilted sensor feels part of gravity as a false acceleration. That is why the IMU is used together with the wheel encoders.

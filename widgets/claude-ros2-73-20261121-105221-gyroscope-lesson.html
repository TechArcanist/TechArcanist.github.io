Chapter 73: the gyroscope

Gyroscope How a spinning disc, two springs and a tiny chip tell a robot which way it turns

Chapter 72 showed that a robot gets a better answer by combining several sensors. This page looks at one of them, the gyroscope, which gives the orientation and the tilt of the robot. It is used together with the wheel encoders to reduce their noise and errors. The physics starts from zero: a twist (torque), a spinning disc, the surprising way a spinning disc reacts, and how two springs turn that into a measurement. Every number is an example, built step by step.

What a gyroscope is for

A gyroscope tells the robot how it is oriented and tilted, and how fast it turns. It keeps its own orientation fixed against a reference frame and feels any push that tries to change it. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. A gyroscope measures the turning of the robot. Adding up the turning speed over time gives the heading: heading = sum of (turning speed × time step). It is used next to the wheel encoders to reduce their noise and measurement errors.

02

A twist on a disc that is not spinning

To see how a gyroscope works, start with a plain disc and a twist. A twist is called a torque. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Torque T = force × distance from the axis. A torque on a disc that is not spinning turns it around the same axis as the torque: angular acceleration = T ÷ I, where I (moment of inertia) says how hard the disc is to turn.

03

The same twist on a spinning disc

Now make the disc spin fast along its own axis and give it the same twist. It does not turn the way the twist points. It turns around a different axis, the third one. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. A torque T on a spinning disc with spin momentum L = I × spin speed makes the spin axis turn at the rate Ω = T ÷ L, around the axis at a right angle to both. The faster the disc spins, the less it turns: it resists changes of its orientation.

04

Springs turn the push into a measurement

To measure the push on the disc, keep its orientation fixed with springs. The twist then squeezes the springs, and the squeeze can be measured. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. The compression of the springs gives the force F = k × compression, the force gives the torque T = F × d, and the torque is T = L × Ω, so the compression tells how fast the robot turns: Ω = k × compression × d ÷ L.

05

Today's gyroscopes: a chip and three angles

Gyroscopes are now so small that they are about the size of a coin, and often one chip measures the turning around all three axes at once: roll, pitch and yaw. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. Roll, pitch and yaw are the three turns in space: around the forward axis, the sideways axis and the vertical axis. A robot on a flat floor only changes its yaw, so only that measurement is used. A drone moves freely in three dimensions, so all three matter to know how it is tilted and oriented.

06

Strengths and limits

A gyroscope is good at quick turns and is not fooled by slipping wheels. But every sensor has errors, and adding up small errors has a price. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. A gyroscope is not affected by wheel slip, but a small constant error (a bias) in its reading is added up too and grows with time: heading error = bias × time. The wheel encoders fail in other ways, so using both together is better than either alone.

07

Summary and quiz

Key takeaways

  1. A gyroscope measures how the robot turns. It is used with the wheel encoders to reduce their noise and measurement errors.
  2. Adding up the turning speed times the time step gives the heading, for example 30 degrees per second for 4 seconds gives 120 degrees.
  3. Torque T = force × distance from the axis. A torque on a disc that is not spinning turns it around the same axis.
  4. On a spinning disc with spin momentum L = I × spin speed, a torque turns the spin axis at Ω = T ÷ L, around the third axis. Faster spin means a smaller turn.
  5. Springs that hold the orientation fixed are compressed by the torque. Compression gives force, force gives torque, torque gives how fast the robot turns.
  6. Modern gyroscopes are as small as a coin and measure roll, pitch and yaw. A robot on a plane uses only yaw, a drone uses all three.
  7. A gyroscope is not fooled by wheel slip, but a constant bias is added up: 0.5 degrees per second becomes 30 degrees in a minute. That is why sensors are used together.

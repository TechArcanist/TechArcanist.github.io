Chapter 73: the gyroscope

Gyroscope How a spinning disc, two springs and a tiny chip tell a robot how it turns

A gyroscope tells a robot how it turns and tilts. This page shows why the wheel encoders need that help, then opens the gyroscope step by step: a twist on a still disc, the same twist on a spinning disc, and springs that turn the push into a number. Last, today's coin sized chips and the three angles roll, pitch and yaw.

What a gyroscope is and how it works

Why the wheel encoders need help, what a gyroscope measures, and how a spinning disc and two springs measure the turn of the robot. Use Next and Back, the sliders and the buttons. Point at a coloured word to light up its part of the picture.

Key idea. A gyroscope measures the orientation and the tilt of the robot and helps the wheel encoders. Inside, a twist turns a still disc around the twist axis, but turns a spinning disc around the third axis. Springs hold the spinning disc: their squeeze gives the force, the torque and so the turn of the robot.

Summary and quiz

Key takeaways

  1. A gyroscope determines the orientation and the tilt of the robot. Next to the wheel encoders it improves the odometry and reduces their noise and errors.
  2. It keeps its own direction fixed against a reference frame and measures any push that tries to change it.
  3. A torque on a still disc turns it around the same axis as the torque.
  4. The same torque on a spinning disc turns it around the third axis.
  5. Springs keep the orientation fixed. Their squeeze gives the force, the force gives the torque, the torque gives the change of orientation.
  6. Today a gyroscope is a chip not bigger than a coin, measuring roll, pitch and yaw. A ground robot uses only the yaw, a drone all three.
